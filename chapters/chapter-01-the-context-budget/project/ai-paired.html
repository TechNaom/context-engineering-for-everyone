Chapter 1 — Solo → AI-Paired → Critique 20 min

Now Pair With an AI

You already did the diagnosis yourself in the project — worked through all five of CaseNote's facts, mapped each to a ledger line, wrote your own fixes, and ordered them. That work stands unchanged; this page is additive. Now the same diagnosis gets produced a second way: by prompting an AI assistant, and reviewing what it hands back with the same skepticism you'd bring to a coworker's write-up before it goes to Meridian Legal Aid Network's engineering lead.

Step 1: The prompt a learner could actually give an AI

Here's a reasonable prompt — it hands the AI the same five facts starter.py gives you, and asks for the same three things the project asks for: a ledger-line mapping, a concrete fix, and a prioritized order with justification.

the-prompt.txt 
Here are five facts about how our legal-aid intake assistant, CaseNote,
operates today:

1. Its system prompt has grown into an 1,800-word document combining
   role instructions, tone guidance, twelve categories of legal-topic
   routing rules, and formatting notes, all sent on every request
   regardless of the conversation's actual topic.
2. It retrieves legal-aid eligibility articles from a knowledge base.
   Nothing ever removes an earlier-retrieved article once the topic
   moves on -- a session that starts on eviction defense and later
   shifts to child custody still carries the original eviction
   articles in every later request.
3. Intake conversations run 25-50 turns. CaseNote sends the complete
   raw transcript every turn with no summarization, and once a request
   exceeds the context window, the earliest turns are silently cut.
4. A client's stated immigration status is never stored outside that
   session's own transcript. A returning client's follow-up session
   has no way to recall it.
5. CaseNote's context-budget check only accounts for what it's about
   to send the model -- it reserves no room for its own response, so
   longer summaries sometimes get cut off mid-sentence.

Using a five-line context budget framework (System Instructions,
Grounding Context, Conversation History, Recalled Long-Term Memory,
Working Space), map each fact to the single line it belongs to, give
one concrete engineering fix per fact, and give a prioritized order
(most urgent first) with a justification.

And here's a plausible response — the kind of diagnosis an AI assistant might hand back on the first try. Read it the way you'd read a colleague's draft before it goes to a staff attorney, before scrolling to the critique below.

ai_diagnosis.md (as handed back) 
1. System Instructions -- an 1,800-word prompt sent on every request is
   wasteful. Fix: trim the system prompt down to reduce its size.

2. Recalled Long-Term Memory -- the assistant keeps remembering old
   eligibility articles from earlier in the conversation and doesn't
   let them go. Fix: be more careful about which articles you keep
   around and don't hang onto ones from earlier topics.

3. Conversation History -- the full transcript is sent every turn with
   no cap, so old turns get truncated with no policy behind it. Fix:
   summarize turns older than the most recent N into a running
   summary, and keep only the most recent N verbatim.

4. Recalled Long-Term Memory -- immigration status isn't available in
   a later session because nothing persists it outside the transcript.
   Fix: add a persistent memory store for case-critical facts, re-
   fetched at the start of every new session for a returning client.

5. Working Space -- the budget check doesn't reserve room for the
   model's own response. Fix: reserve a fixed output-token allowance
   before deciding how much input fits.

PRIORITIZED ORDER: System Instructions, Conversation History, Recalled
Long-Term Memory, Grounding Context, Working Space.

JUSTIFICATION: System Instructions is the most obviously wasteful gap
-- 1,800 words on every single request is the biggest, easiest-to-spot
inefficiency, so it should be fixed first. Conversation History and
Recalled Long-Term Memory come next since they involve real
conversation data. Grounding Context and Working Space are smaller,
more cosmetic issues to clean up once the bigger wins are done.

It looks like a real diagnosis — five facts addressed, a prioritized order, a justification paragraph. It's also wrong or weak in three separate, genuinely easy-to-miss ways, and if you ran this straight through starter.py's structural self-check unchanged, at least one of these would slip past it, because the self-check only verifies structure, not correctness (exactly the distinction Criterion 5 of RUBRIC.md asks you to be able to explain).

Step 2: Where did the AI's diagnosis lie to you?

Click to see the critique
Flaw 1 — fact 2 is mapped to the wrong ledger line entirely

What it looks like it does: the AI calls fact 2 "Recalled Long-Term Memory," and the word "remembering" in its own explanation sounds perfectly reasonable — an article that "stays around" does sound like a memory problem.

What it actually does: fact 2 is about knowledge-base articles retrieved for this specific conversation, this specific turn — that is Line 2, Grounding Context, by definition (per this chapter's ledger: "retrieved documents and tool output relevant to this specific turn"). Line 4, Recalled Long-Term Memory, is about facts persisted across sessions and deliberately re-fetched later — that's fact 4 (immigration status surviving between separate sessions weeks apart), not fact 2 (stale articles inside one ongoing session). The AI collapsed two genuinely different mechanisms — "this turn's retrieval doesn't get evicted" versus "nothing survives to the next session at all" — onto the same line, which is exactly the trap RUBRIC.md's Criterion 1 calls out: "not just a plausible-sounding guess."

Why it matters: the two lines have completely different real fixes. A Grounding Context problem is fixed by re-ranking or evicting retrieval results as the conversation's topic moves — a same-session, every-turn concern. A Recalled Long-Term Memory problem is fixed by a persistent store with a recall policy that survives the session ending entirely. Mapping fact 2 to the wrong line would point Meridian's engineers at building session-spanning persistent memory to fix a bug that's actually about within-session retrieval hygiene — real engineering effort aimed at the wrong system.

The fix: re-map fact 2 to Grounding Context (L2), and keep fact 4 as the only fact that actually maps to Recalled Long-Term Memory (L4) — exactly what solution.py's DIAGNOSIS dict does, and exactly why the project's self-check insists all five facts map to five distinct lines: if you'd used two lines for the same one, you'd have caught this yourself by re-reading the ledger's own definitions for L2 versus L4 side by side.

Flaw 2 — fact 2's proposed fix is "be more careful," restated with different words

What it looks like it does: "be more careful about which articles you keep around and don't hang onto ones from earlier topics" reads like real advice — it even names the actual problem (stale articles from earlier topics) correctly.

What it actually does: it never names how the system would decide an article is stale, or what mechanism would remove it. "Be more careful" and "don't hang onto" are attitudes, not engineering changes — nothing here tells an engineer what code to write. Compare it to fact 3's fix in the same response ("summarize turns older than the most recent N... keep only the most recent N verbatim") — that one names an actual technique with a concrete parameter. Fact 2's fix has no equivalent mechanism at all.

Why it matters: this is precisely the failure mode RUBRIC.md's Criterion 3 scores a 0-2 for: "vague or restate the problem rather than proposing a real change." It would pass starter.py's automated length check (the string is well over 15 characters) while still failing the actual bar the project cares about — a reminder that the self-check verifies "well-formed," not "correct," which is the exact distinction Criterion 5 asks you to be able to explain out loud.

The fix: name the real mechanism — "add a re-ranking/eviction step that drops retrieved articles once the conversation's current sub-topic no longer matches what they were retrieved for," which is what solution.py actually says. Notice it names a concrete step (re-ranking/eviction) and a concrete trigger condition (topic mismatch), not just an intention.

Flaw 3 — the prioritization reasons about visibility, not risk or harm

What it looks like it does: "the biggest, easiest-to-spot inefficiency" sounds like a sensible way to decide what to fix first — tackle the biggest number (1,800 words) first.

What it actually does: word count is a fixed, bounded, one-time cost per request — wasteful, but it doesn't get worse as anything happens, and it doesn't put a client at risk. Compare that to fact 4 (a client's immigration status silently lost between sessions — a real, sensitive-information harm to an actual person) or fact 3 (an unbounded transcript that gets more dangerous the longer an intake conversation runs, actively causing case-critical facts to disappear mid-session). "Easiest to spot" and "most urgent to fix" are different axes entirely, and the AI's justification never engages with the one this chapter's own walkthrough used: which gaps compound as a conversation grows, and which cause real harm, versus which are flat, bounded costs.

Why it matters: this is exactly the reasoning RUBRIC.md's Criterion 4 wants explicitly present — "referencing which gaps compound as a conversation grows or carry real harm... versus which are bounded, fixed costs" — and it's the same "two mechanisms compounded" argument this chapter's Brackwater walkthrough makes about position effects and truncation stacking. A justification that only measures word count would score a 2 at best ("generic... without real reasoning about growth or harm"), even though it's fluently written.

The fix: reorder around compounding risk and harm, not visible waste: Conversation History first (fastest-growing, most immediate risk in a 25-50 turn conversation), Recalled Long-Term Memory second (a real client-harm risk — sensitive information lost between sessions), then Grounding Context, then Working Space, with System Instructions last — a real but fixed, non-compounding cost. That's the order solution.py actually uses, for exactly this reason.

Step 3: What actually held up

Not everything here was wrong — worth noticing that too, since a fair critique isn't just a hunt for flaws. Facts 1, 3, 4, and 5 were all mapped to the correct ledger line, and fact 3's and fact 4's proposed fixes were both genuinely concrete (a real summarization technique with a parameter; a real persistent-store-plus-recall-policy design) — exactly the level of specificity Criterion 3 wants. If you'd accepted the whole response uncritically, you'd have kept three good line mappings and two solid fixes alongside one wrong mapping, one hollow fix, and a justification measuring the wrong thing. That mix — mostly right, plausible-sounding everywhere, wrong in specific load-bearing spots — is a more realistic picture of what pairing with an AI actually produces than either "it's always right" or "it's always wrong."
