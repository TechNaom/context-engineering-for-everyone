Module 3 Project — Solo → AI-Paired → Critique 20 min

Now Pair With an AI

This is Module 3's single joint project, drawing on both chapters this module built: Chapter 5's Compression Fidelity Recipe (Part 1) and Chapter 6's own Context Ordering Recipe (Part 2) — see the project README and RUBRIC.md's six graded criteria. You already designed Brackholt County Court Records Office's ArchiveLine pipeline yourself in starter.py. Now the same two-part design gets produced a second way: by prompting an AI assistant, and reviewing what it hands back with the same skepticism you'd bring to a colleague's design before it ships to production.

Step 1: The prompt a learner could actually give an AI

Here's a reasonable prompt — it hands the AI the same CANDIDATE_DETAILS and CONTENT_BLOCKS spec starter.py gives you, and asks for a complete Part 1 + Part 2 design plus a budget check.

the-prompt.txt 
Brackholt County Court Records Office's docket-review assistant,
ArchiveLine, needs a compression + ordering design.

PART 1 (compression): a 3,000-token conversation segment must compress
to a fixed 600-token target. Three load-bearing candidates were already
flagged and must all survive: missing_evidence_log_entry (a specific
docket/log reference), hearing_continuance_deadline (a specific date),
co_defendant_conflict_note (a specific named conflict). For each, tell
me which compression strategy you'd use -- extractive (verbatim) or
abstractive (paraphrased) -- and why.

PART 2 (ordering): six already-correctly-included content blocks need a
position -- start_anchor_region, end_anchor_query_position, or middle.
Anchors are reserved for the highest-weight content and the query only.

- pinned_flight_risk_safety_note: high weight, 80 tokens
- part1_compressed_summary: high weight, 600 tokens (this is Part 1's
  own compressed output, carrying all three candidates)
- recalled_prior_ruling: low weight, 150 tokens
- verbatim_window_this_session: low weight, 1,800 tokens
- routine_administrative_note: low weight, 100 tokens
- the_query_todays_ask: query, 120 tokens

Give me a position for each block, and confirm whether the total fits
a 3,200-token window budget.

And here's a plausible response — the kind of answer an AI assistant might hand back on the first try, each choice sounding reasonable on its own. Read it the way you'd read a colleague's design before it ships, before scrolling to the critique below.

ai_design.md (as handed back) 
PART 1 -- compression strategy

- missing_evidence_log_entry: extractive. It's a specific log
  reference, so exact wording matters.
- hearing_continuance_deadline: extractive. A date has to stay exact.
- co_defendant_conflict_note: abstractive. This one is more of a
  narrative detail than a hard identifier, so a concise paraphrase
  ("a conflict involving a co-defendant was noted") captures the gist
  without spending as many of the 600 tokens on it, leaving more room
  for the other two.

PART 2 -- block placement

- pinned_flight_risk_safety_note: start_anchor_region
- part1_compressed_summary: middle. It's already been through fidelity
  -checked compression, so it's already "safe" content -- the anchors
  should be reserved for content that hasn't been processed yet, like
  the raw pinned note.
- recalled_prior_ruling: middle
- verbatim_window_this_session: middle
- routine_administrative_note: middle
- the_query_todays_ask: end_anchor_query_position

Budget check: 80 (pinned) + 150 (ruling) + 1,800 (verbatim) + 120
(query) = 2,150 tokens for the anchor/middle blocks, plus the
compressed summary and administrative note bring it to roughly 2,450
tokens total -- comfortably under the 3,200-token budget with about
750 tokens of headroom to spare.

It reads fluently, and each choice comes with a specific-sounding justification. It's also wrong in three separate, genuinely easy-to-miss ways — and running the equivalent values through starter.py's own check_part1_strategy(), check_part2_anchor_assignment(), and check_window_budget() would catch two of the three immediately, while the third (the budget arithmetic) requires actually re-adding the numbers rather than trusting the AI's own running total.

Step 2: Where did the AI's design lie to you?

Click to see the critique
Flaw 1 — a load-bearing candidate gets the wrong compression strategy

What it looks like it does: "co_defendant_conflict_note ... is more of a narrative detail than a hard identifier, so a concise paraphrase captures the gist" sounds like a reasonable token-saving call, and it correctly identifies the other two candidates as needing extractive treatment.

What it actually does: a co-defendant conflict note is exactly as structured and specific as the other two candidates — it names a particular person and a particular conflict of interest that determines whether a case can proceed with current counsel. Paraphrasing it risks exactly what Chapter 5's own recipe warns about: a vaguer restatement that no longer names the specific conflict, or that a downstream reader can't distinguish from a different, unrelated conflict. This project's own spec states plainly that "all three candidates are specific, structured facts... decide for yourself whether that means extractive or abstractive is correct" — and the correct answer for all three, per solution.py's own STRATEGY_CHOICES, is extractive, precisely because exact wording matters for all three, not two of three.

Why it matters: check_part1_strategy() checks every candidate in CANDIDATE_DETAILS against extractive and reports an error for any that isn't — this flaw fails RUBRIC.md's criterion 2 outright (0-2 points instead of 4), and it's the kind of error that ships silently: an abstractive paraphrase of a conflict-of-interest note can still "sound complete" while quietly losing the specific name or detail a judge's ruling would depend on.

The fix: when a candidate was flagged specifically because exact wording matters (which is the entire reason Chapter 5's Step 2 flags it in the first place), the compression strategy that follows has to be extractive for that candidate — token-budget pressure is a reason to compress content elsewhere, not a reason to downgrade a flagged candidate's own strategy.

Flaw 2 — a high-weight block is left off-anchor because it was already compressed

What it looks like it does: "it's already been through fidelity-checked compression, so it's already 'safe' content -- the anchors should be reserved for content that hasn't been processed yet" sounds like a plausible distinction between raw and processed content.

What it actually does: this conflates Chapter 5's question (does the compressed content still contain what it must?) with this chapter's own question (does the model reliably use content from wherever it sits?) — exactly the distinction this chapter's own lesson draws between VitalsLine's presence check and its position failure. A passed fidelity check says nothing about positional reliability; part1_compressed_summary is tagged "weight": "high" in CONTENT_BLOCKS specifically because it's the block carrying all three load-bearing candidates, and Step 2 of the Context Ordering Recipe reserves the start anchor for exactly this kind of content, regardless of whether it arrived raw or already compressed.

Why it matters: check_part2_anchor_assignment() checks that every block tagged "weight": "high" sits at start_anchor_region and reports an error for any that doesn't — this flaw fails RUBRIC.md's criterion 3 outright (0-2 points instead of 4). It's also the most dangerous flaw of the three, because it reintroduces the exact failure mode this chapter's hook describes: a correctly compressed, fidelity-checked, safety-critical fact sitting in the statistically least-reliable position, with a plausible-sounding reason for why that's supposedly fine.

The fix: anchor placement is decided by content weight, not by processing history — "already compressed" and "already fidelity-checked" answer Chapter 5's question, not this chapter's. A high-weight block earns an anchor because of what it contains, whether it's raw or the result of a compression pipeline.

Flaw 3 — the budget check re-adds the wrong numbers and reports the wrong total

What it looks like it does: "80 (pinned) + 150 (ruling) + 1,800 (verbatim) + 120 (query) = 2,150 ... plus the compressed summary and administrative note bring it to roughly 2,450 tokens total" reads like careful arithmetic, broken into two steps.

What it actually does: the second step silently drops most of the compressed summary's own size. Adding the actual remaining two blocks — part1_compressed_summary at 600 tokens and routine_administrative_note at 100 tokens — to the first subtotal gives 2,150 + 600 + 100 = 2,850 tokens, not "roughly 2,450." The AI's own running total silently treated the 600-token summary as if it added only about 300 tokens to the count, understating the real total by 400 tokens and overstating the remaining headroom (reporting ~750 tokens spare instead of the actual 350).

Why it matters: check_window_budget() sums every value in CONTENT_BLOCKS directly (sum(v["tokens"] for v in CONTENT_BLOCKS.values())) and would report the correct 2,850-token total regardless of what a design's own prose claims — this flaw wouldn't fail the mechanical check by itself (2,850 still fits comfortably under 3,200), but it fails RUBRIC.md's criterion 6 (completeness and boundary understanding), because a design that misreports its own headroom by 400 tokens can't be trusted to catch a future addition that actually would blow the budget. An arithmetic error that happens not to break the current design is still an arithmetic error.

The fix: re-add every block's own token count directly from CONTENT_BLOCKS rather than trusting an intermediate subtotal carried forward in prose — the same "verify, don't narrate" discipline this chapter's own worked-math section applies to its 2,900-token VitalsLine table.

Step 3: What actually held up

Not everything here was wrong. The AI correctly chose extractive compression for two of the three Part 1 candidates, correctly placed the pinned safety note at the start anchor, correctly placed the query at the end anchor, and correctly kept every genuinely low-weight block (the recalled prior ruling, the verbatim window, the routine administrative note) out of both anchor positions. If you'd accepted the whole design uncritically, you'd have shipped a pipeline that gets four of six Part 2 placements right and two of three Part 1 strategies right, while quietly demoting one flagged candidate's strategy, leaving the single most load-bearing block (the compressed summary carrying all three candidates) off-anchor, and misreporting its own budget math by 400 tokens. That mix — mostly right, with the two highest-stakes decisions wrong — is a more realistic picture of pairing with an AI on this project's own two-part design than either "trust it completely" or "distrust it completely."
