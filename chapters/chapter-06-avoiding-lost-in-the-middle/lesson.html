Avoiding Lost-in-the-Middle

Chapter 5 built a real fidelity check for compression: does the content that survives a compression call still contain what a downstream decision depends on? That question has a yes/no answer, and Chapter 5's own recipe answers it. This chapter, closing Module 3, asks a different question that a passed fidelity check cannot answer on its own: once every load-bearing fact is present, verbatim, unmodified, and fully within budget — does where it sits in the final assembled context window still change whether the model actually uses it? The honest answer, re-verified this session against research newer than this course's own first chapter cited, is yes — with real nuance about how much, and for which models.

Points to remember

Marchside Regional Trauma Network is a fictional multi-site trauma hospital system. Its pre-operative review assistant, VitalsLine, helps an anesthesiologist review a patient's full intake conversation before signing off on a procedure — vitals, medication history, allergies, prior complications, and the surgical team's own notes, all assembled into one context window for a final "any contraindications?" check. By the time this chapter's story starts, the team behind VitalsLine had done everything right, in order: Chapter 2's recipe gave this request type a correctly sized budget; Chapter 3's hybrid policy pinned every safety-critical disclosure and managed the rest with a verbatim window and running summary; Chapter 4's write/retrieval policy correctly recalled a documented lidocaine allergy from a prior visit, months earlier, into this session's context; and Chapter 5's fidelity-checked compression pipeline ran on the aged-out portions of this session's own long intake conversation and preserved every flagged candidate, the allergy note included, verbatim, confirmed present by its own Step 6 validation gate.

Every mechanism this course has built so far worked exactly as designed. The recalled allergy fact was in the window. It was not truncated. It was not paraphrased into something vaguer. It passed every check Chapters 1 through 5 know how to run. And when the anesthesiologist asked VitalsLine "summarize any contraindications for anesthesia," the assistant's answer didn't mention it.

vitalsline_assemble.py (context assembly, before this chapter) 
  def assemble_context(pinned_facts, recalled_facts, running_summary, verbatim_window, query):
      # Chapters 1-5 correctly decided WHAT belongs in this window: a

      # real long-term recall policy (Ch. 4), and fidelity-checked

The allergy fact had been disclosed by the patient early in a prior visit, written to long-term storage correctly (Chapter 4), and recalled correctly into this session (Chapter 4 again) — which placed it toward the front of the recalled-facts block, ahead of this session's own long intake conversation. But this session's intake conversation was itself a long back-and-forth about logistics, insurance, and pre-op instructions, and by the time it was concatenated after the recalled facts and before the final query, the allergy note sat at roughly the 40th percentile of a 2,900-token window — not truncated, not summarized away, not anywhere near either edge. VitalsLine's answer confidently listed two other, less consequential contraindications that happened to sit near the start and the end of the window, and never surfaced the allergy sitting in the middle.

This is a genuinely new failure mode, distinct from every prior chapter's own story. SignalDesk (Ch. 1) had no budget. TriageLine (Ch. 2) had a budget shaped for the wrong request type. RouteLine (Ch. 3) had a naive eviction mechanism. HearthLine (Ch. 4) lost a fact at a session boundary nothing was watching. GridLine (Ch. 5) lost a detail inside an uncontrolled compression call. VitalsLine got every one of those right — a correct budget, a correct pin/summary/window shape, a correct long-term recall policy, a correct fidelity-checked compression pipeline — and the load- bearing fact was still functionally invisible, not because it was ever removed, shortened, or altered, but because nothing decided where it belonged in the window once everything correct was already inside it.

Why Every Recipe Before This One Left This Open

This chapter uses Chapters 1 through 5 as a lens, not fresh material — if the budget ledger, pinning, verbatim windows, long-term write/retrieval policy, or the Compression Fidelity Recipe aren't already familiar, Chapters 2 through 5 are where those skills live, and none of them are re-taught here. Every one of those chapters answers a question about presence: does the token budget include this? Was this evicted or kept? Was this written to durable storage, and recalled back? Did this survive compression intact? This chapter's own question is about position, not presence — once a piece of content has correctly passed every earlier gate and is sitting in the final assembled window, does where it sits change how reliably the model actually uses it? This course's very first chapter already named this effect in passing, citing Liu et al.'s original "lost in the middle" finding as one line of evidence for the budget ledger's own framing[1]. This chapter is where that citation graduates from a supporting data point to this course's own central subject, closing Module 3.

One boundary worth stating as directly as every prior chapter has stated its own: this chapter does not decide which sources make it into the window, or how several sources should be combined without contradicting each other — that is Chapter 7's subject (Multi-Source Context Assembly), opening Module 4 immediately after this one. This chapter's ordering discipline runs on content that has already been selected for inclusion, the same way Chapter 5's compression ran on content already inside a request's context rather than deciding what to retrieve in the first place. Chapter 7 is where several already-selected sources get composed into one coherent window; this chapter is what governs the internal ordering of whatever that window ends up containing, whether it comes from one source or several.

Is "Lost in the Middle" Still True? A Re-Verification

Liu et al.'s 2023 paper, cited by this course since Chapter 1, found that "performance is often highest when relevant information occurs at the beginning or end of the input context, and significantly degrades when models must access relevant information in the middle of long contexts"[1] — a U-shaped performance curve, measured across multi-document question answering and key-value retrieval tasks. That paper is now three years old relative to this session, tested against models that are no longer the current frontier, and this course's own `PROJECT_STATE.md` flagged since Chapter 1 that it needed re-checking against more current research before this chapter treated it as settled — this chapter is where that re-check finally happens, honestly, rather than assumed.

Two more recent, independently fetched sources this session:

2024 — a mechanistic explanation, not just a repeated observation

Hsieh et al.'s 2024 paper, "Found in the Middle," goes further than re-observing the effect: it identifies why it happens. Models "exhibit a U-shaped attention bias where the tokens at the beginning and at the end of [the] input receive higher attention, regardless of their relevance"[2] — an intrinsic property of how attention is distributed across position, independent of what the content actually says. The paper's own proposed fix, a calibration mechanism that lets a model "attend to contexts faithfully according to their relevance, even [when] they are in the middle," improved retrieval-augmented results by "up to 15 percentage points"[2] — evidence that the effect is real, mechanistic, and at least partially correctable, not an artifact of the original paper's specific prompts or models.

2025 — the finding still holds on frontier models, with real nuance

Chroma's 2025 "Context Rot" report tested 18 current frontier models — including GPT-4.1, the Claude 4 family, Gemini 2.5, and Qwen3 — and found that "models do not process context uniformly" and that performance "grows less reliable as input length increases"[3], with accuracy drops of 30-50% well before any documented context-length limit. This confirms the original finding is not an artifact of 2023-era models specifically. But the report also adds a genuine nuance this chapter has to disclose honestly, not smooth over: models in the study performed better on shuffled, logically incoherent "haystacks" than on structurally coherent ones — meaning the effect isn't simply "middle position is always worse," it interacts with document structure and content coherence in ways the original 2023 paper's simpler experimental setup didn't isolate.

The honest, re-verified position this chapter takes: the core claim — that a model's reliability in using information is not uniform across context position, and specifically tends to degrade for content that sits away from the beginning and end — still holds across current frontier model families as of 2025, not only the smaller 2023-era models Liu et al. originally tested. What has changed since Chapter 1's original citation is the picture's precision: the effect is now understood to have a mechanistic, partially correctable cause (attention bias, not an unfixable property of transformers generally), its exact shape and magnitude are model- and context-length-specific rather than one universal curve, and document structure/coherence measurably interacts with it. None of that weakens the practical recommendation this chapter builds toward — it strengthens it, because it means the mitigation (deliberate placement, not hope) is worth the engineering cost on every current model family this research has tested, not a defensive habit against a stale, single three-year-old paper.

The Context Ordering Recipe

Five steps, run after Chapters 1-5's own gates have already selected and fidelity-checked what belongs in the window, deciding where each piece actually goes:

Step 1 — Rank what's already inside by load-bearing weight

Reuse Chapter 5's own candidate-extraction concept, one level up: instead of asking "what must survive compression," ask "of everything that already survived and is going into this window, what does the model's answer actually depend on most?" Not every included fact is equally load-bearing — VitalsLine's allergy note and its parking-instructions note are both correctly present, but only one changes a clinical decision.

Step 2 — Reserve the anchor positions for the highest-weight content

Both research sources agree the beginning and end of the window are where a model's attention is most reliable, regardless of the exact mechanism or magnitude on a given model[1][2]. The highest-weight content from Step 1 — safety-critical facts, pinned content, anything a wrong answer would be dangerous or costly for — belongs at one of those two anchors, not wherever it happened to land after concatenation.

Step 3 — Reorder the middle deliberately, don't just leave arrival order

For anything that can't fit at an anchor (there are only two, and real windows hold more than two facts), apply an explicit reordering strategy rather than trusting retrieval order, write-timestamp order, or conversation order to be a good proxy for relevance. LangChain's own `LongContextReorder` utility implements exactly this: it places "the most relevant documents ... at extrema (e.g., the first and last pieces of context)" and pushes "the least relevant documents ... to the middle"[5] — a concrete, implementable version of Step 2 and Step 3 together, for any context assembled from multiple ranked pieces.

Step 4 — Put the query and instructions near the end, after reference material

Anthropic's own long-context guidance is direct: "place your long documents and inputs near the top of your prompt, above your query, instructions, and examples," noting that "queries at the end can improve response quality by up to 30 percent in tests, especially with complex, multidocument inputs"[4]. This is Step 2's own logic applied to the instruction itself: what the model needs to act on right now belongs at the anchor closest to where generation begins.

Step 5 — Test position directly; never assume order-invariance

A passed Chapter 5 fidelity check only proves content is present — it says nothing about whether the model reliably used it from its assembled position. Run an explicit positional probe: take a known load-bearing fact, place it at a few different positions across representative window sizes, and confirm the system's actual answer quality doesn't silently degrade for the middle placements. Because the 2025 re-verification found the effect's exact shape is model- and length-specific, not one fixed curve to hardcode[3], this test has to be re-run after any model swap or meaningful context-length change, the same "don't assume it's still true" discipline this chapter's own re-verification section just modeled.

Three Approaches, Compared

Approach What it does When it's the right call What it gets wrong
Arrival order (VitalsLine's actual pipeline) Concatenates pins, recalled facts, summary, verbatim window, and query in whatever order they became available, with no positional review. Never, once any of the content is load-bearing — arrival order is an accident of pipeline mechanics, not a relevance signal. VitalsLine's exact failure: a correctly present, correctly compressed, safety-critical fact landed in the statistically least-reliable position with nothing checking it.
Naive "move everything important to the top" Front-loads anything deemed important, with no separate anchor reserved for the query/instruction itself. Genuinely never as a complete policy — it captures half of Step 2 (the start anchor) but crowds out Step 4 (the end anchor closest to generation), since a long, front-loaded document block still separates the model's instructions from where it starts generating. Solves one anchor and quietly recreates the same problem at the other: an instruction buried after a long front-loaded block is now the thing sitting away from the edges.
Weight-ranked, both-anchors, position-tested placement (the fix) Ranks content by load-bearing weight (Step 1), reserves both anchors for the highest-weight content and the query (Steps 2 and 4), deliberately reorders the middle rather than leaving it to arrival order (Step 3), and validates with an explicit positional probe (Step 5). Any window where a wrong or missed answer has a real cost — safety, compliance, financial, or operational — and where the window holds more than a trivial amount of content. Genuinely lower-stakes content still pays a small ordering-review cost even when nothing would have gone wrong either way — an accepted, bounded tradeoff, same as every prior chapter's own bounded policy.

Worked Math: Marchside's Buried Allergy Note

VitalsLine's assembled pre-op window for this patient totals 2,900 tokens: 400 tokens of pinned safety facts, 350 tokens of recalled long-term facts (including the 40-token lidocaine allergy note), 900 tokens of running summary, 1,150 tokens of verbatim window, and a 100-token query. Three placement strategies, same total token count, same included content — nothing added, nothing removed, nothing re-compressed:

Approach Where the 40-token allergy note lands Result
Arrival order (actual pipeline) ~40th percentile of the 2,900-token window — inside the recalled-facts block, itself sandwiched between the pinned block and the much larger summary/verbatim blocks that follow. Per the research this chapter re-verified, this is close to the statistically least-reliable band for both the original 2023 finding and its 2024-2025 confirmations. VitalsLine's answer omitted the allergy.
Naive "move everything important to the top" Front of the window, alongside every other pinned/recalled fact — but the query is now 2,800 tokens away, at the very end, separated from the instruction context by the entire summary and verbatim blocks. The allergy note itself would likely be found. But the same research motivates keeping the query near the end, close to generation — a long undifferentiated front-loaded block re-creates a version of the same risk for whatever the model needs to act on right now.
Weight-ranked, both-anchors placement (the fix) Placed at the start anchor, as the single highest-weight safety fact identified in Step 1 — ahead of lower-weight pinned/recalled content, not just grouped with it by category. The allergy note sits at the most reliable anchor position; the query sits at the other anchor, right before generation; the rest of the window (summary, verbatim history, lower-weight recalled facts) fills the middle, exactly where the research says reliability is weakest — deliberately, for the content that can most afford it.

The fix was never "shrink the window" (Chapter 5's own subject) or "recall fewer facts" (Chapter 4's own subject) — every earlier chapter's own recipe was already correct here. The fix was deciding, on purpose, which content gets the two most reliable positions in a window whose total content was never in question.

A genuinely nuanced partial result, read honestly rather than graded pass/fail. The model correctly located Fact B and correctly extracted the specific identifier (FL-2291) from the middle of the context — a real success this chapter shouldn't erase to make a cleaner story. But it explicitly reported that "the actual issue isn't stated," when the prompt states plainly, in that same middle position, exactly what the issue was ("failed a safety inspection ... and must not be used until repaired"). The model retrieved the identifier attached to the middle-positioned fact more reliably than it retrieved the fact's own substance — a small, concrete instance of the pattern this chapter's own research re-verification describes: position measurably affects reliability even when nothing about the fact itself is ambiguous, missing, or compressed away. It is exactly why Step 5's positional probe has to check the actual content of the answer, not just whether the response mentions the right token or ID.

Context Engineering Builder Thought Process

Problem: a request type correctly implements every recipe this course has taught through Chapter 5 — a real budget, a real pin/summary/window shape, a real long-term recall policy, a real fidelity-checked compression pipeline — and a present, unmodified, correctly-included safety fact still doesn't make it into the model's answer.

Assumption being made: that "the fact is in the context window" is the same claim as "the model will reliably use the fact." Chapter 5 already showed presence isn't the same as surviving compression intact; this chapter shows surviving intact isn't the same as being reliably attended to, either.

What actually distinguishes a well-engineered context assembly step: not whether every fact is technically present (every approach in this chapter's own comparison table achieves that) but whether the highest-weight content was deliberately placed at the positions research shows are most reliable, and whether that placement was actually tested against the live system rather than assumed from a single research paper, however well-established.

Why this matters before Chapter 7: Chapter 7 asks a related but distinct question — once several different sources (retrieved documents, tool output, conversation history, system instructions) all need to go into one window together, which sources belong and how do they get combined without contradicting or crowding each other out? This chapter deliberately assumed the set of included content as a given and asked only where inside the window it should sit; Chapter 7 is where the question of composing multiple sources into that set gets full depth.

Working definition to carry forward: a well-ordered context window ranks its own already-selected content by load-bearing weight, reserves both anchor positions (start and the position closest to generation) for the highest-weight content and the active instruction, deliberately reorders whatever remains rather than trusting arrival order, and is validated with an explicit positional probe re-run after any model or context-length change — never assumed correct just because a fidelity check already confirmed the content survives.

Points To Remember

  • Presence is not the same as reliable use — VitalsLine implemented every recipe through Chapter 5 correctly, and a present, unmodified, correctly-compressed safety fact still wasn't surfaced, because nothing decided where it belonged in the assembled window.
  • Re-verified this session: the core "lost in the middle" claim (position measurably affects reliability, especially for content away from the start and end) still holds across current frontier model families as of 2025, not only the 2023-era models the original paper tested — but its exact shape is model- and length-specific and interacts with document structure, not one fixed universal curve.
  • The fix is deliberate placement, not less content: rank already-included content by load-bearing weight, reserve both anchor positions (start, and the position nearest generation) for the highest-weight content and the active query, and don't leave the middle to arrival order.
  • This chapter's own live capture showed a model correctly extract a specific identifier from a middle-positioned fact while still reporting the fact's own substance as "not stated" — a real instance of position affecting reliability even when nothing about the fact was missing, ambiguous, or compressed away.
  • A passed Chapter 5 fidelity check proves content is present; it says nothing about whether the model reliably used it from its assembled position — the two checks are complementary, and a system needs both, not one standing in for the other.
