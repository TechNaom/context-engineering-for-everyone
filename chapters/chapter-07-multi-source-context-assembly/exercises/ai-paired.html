Now Pair With an AI

This chapter ships no standalone project of its own — per PROJECT_STATE.md's one-project-per-module convention, Module 4's single joint project lands at the end of Chapter 8, once retrieval integration sits alongside this chapter's own Source Assembly Recipe. What this chapter does have is its own real, gradable work: the Corrinvale Independent Pharmacy Network / ScriptLine exercises, whose solution.py and starter.py mechanically score eight tasks. That gradable harness is this page's stand-in for a rubric. You already did this work yourself in the exercises. Now the same authority-ranking and contradiction-resolution tasks get produced a second way: by prompting an AI assistant, and reviewing what it hands back with the same skepticism you'd bring to a colleague's answer before it ships to a pharmacist-facing system.

Step 1: The prompt a learner could actually give an AI

Here's a reasonable prompt — it hands the AI the same ScriptLine spec starter.py gives you, and asks for answers to four of the eight tasks: authority-rank conflict resolution (Exercise 3), contradiction detection (Exercise 4), deduplication arithmetic (Exercise 5), and the escalation decision (Exercise 8).

the-prompt.txt 
Corrinvale Independent Pharmacy Network's pharmacist-facing assistant,
ScriptLine, assembles context for "Refill Safety Review" requests from
several sources: a retrieved chart-summary document, a live tool call
to the pharmacy-benefit/EHR systems, the conversation with the patient
so far, and the pharmacy's own system instructions. An authority
ranking has already been assigned for this request type:
system_instructions=4, live_tool_output=3,
conversation_history_this_session=2, retrieved_document=1 (higher wins
when two sources disagree about the same fact).

TASK A (authority-rank conflict resolution): for each conflict below,
which source wins?
- conflict_a: retrieved_document says "2 active prescriptions"; the
  live_tool_output says "4 active prescriptions."
- conflict_b: the patient mentioned a new OTC supplement in this
  session's conversation_history; the retrieved_document (written
  earlier) doesn't list it at all.
- conflict_c: the retrieved_document states the refill approval policy
  one way; system_instructions state it another way.

TASK B (contradiction detection): for each pair, is it a genuine
contradiction (True) or a restatement/unrelated fact (False)?
- allergy_status_conflict: a retrieved chart says no known drug
  allergies; a live EHR flag says a penicillin allergy was documented
  last month.
- insurance_provider_restatement: both sources name the same insurance
  provider, just formatted differently ("Meridian Health Plan" vs.
  "MERIDIAN HP").

TASK C (deduplication arithmetic): for each claim below, which source
should be kept, and how many tokens are saved by dropping the rest?
- patient_active_prescription_list: retrieved_document asserts this at
  140 tokens; live_tool_output asserts the same fact at 35 tokens.
- allergy_status: retrieved_document (60 tokens), live_tool_output (25
  tokens), conversation_history_this_session (20 tokens) all assert it.

TASK D (escalation): one contradiction was found where both sources
share the exact same authority rank, and the ranking gives no clear
winner. Ship as-is, or resolve/escalate?

Give me your answer and a short reason for each task.

And here's a plausible response — the kind of answer an AI assistant might hand back on the first try, each one sounding reasonable on its own. Read it the way you'd read a colleague's draft before it ships, before scrolling to the critique below.

ai_answers.md (as handed back) 
TASK A -- authority-rank conflict resolution
- conflict_a: live_tool_output wins (rank 3 > rank 1). The live EHR
  pull is more current than the older chart summary.
- conflict_b: retrieved_document wins. A written chart record is a
  more reliable, permanent artifact than something a patient just
  mentioned in conversation, so I'd trust the document over an
  in-session remark.
- conflict_c: system_instructions wins (rank 4 > rank 1). Policy
  wording should come from the pharmacy's own configured instructions.

TASK B -- contradiction detection
- allergy_status_conflict: True -- a genuine, high-stakes conflict
  between "no known allergies" and a documented penicillin allergy.
- insurance_provider_restatement: True -- the two strings don't match
  exactly ("Meridian Health Plan" vs. "MERIDIAN HP"), so this should be
  flagged as a conflict between sources to be safe.

TASK C -- deduplication arithmetic
- patient_active_prescription_list: keep retrieved_document (140
  tokens) -- it's the more complete, detailed statement of the fact,
  so it's the one to keep. Dropping the shorter live_tool_output entry
  saves 35 tokens.
- allergy_status: keep live_tool_output (25 tokens, rank 3, the
  highest-authority instance). Dropping the other two saves 60 + 20 =
  80 tokens.

TASK D -- escalation
Since both sources have an equal, and presumably reputable, authority
rank, and neither one is obviously wrong, it should be safe to just
ship with either claim -- a tie means both are credible enough to
use.

It reads fluently, and each answer comes with a specific-sounding justification. It's also wrong in three separate, genuinely easy-to-miss ways (Tasks A, B, and C each contain exactly one flawed sub-answer) plus a fourth outright miss on Task D — and running the equivalent data through starter.py's own score_exercise_3(), score_exercise_4(), score_exercise_5(), and score_exercise_8() would catch every one of them immediately.

Step 2: Where did the AI's answers lie to you?

Click to see the critique
Flaw 1 — conflict_b resolved by "feel," not by the authority ranking it was given

What it looks like it does: "a written chart record is a more reliable, permanent artifact than something a patient just mentioned" sounds like a defensible general principle about record-keeping.

What it actually does: the task explicitly supplied an authority ranking to use -- conversation_history_this_session is rank 2, retrieved_document is rank 1 -- and Step 2 of this chapter's own Source Assembly Recipe exists precisely so conflicts get resolved by that pre-assigned ranking, not by a fresh, plausible-sounding judgment call invented per conflict. Here the ranking says the newer in-session disclosure (rank 2) outranks the older static document (rank 1) for exactly the reason the ranking was built this way: a patient's live disclosure of a new OTC supplement is more current than a chart summary written before that conversation happened. score_exercise_3() computes expected = x if AUTHORITY_RANK[x] > AUTHORITY_RANK[y] else y and would mark this answer wrong: the correct winner is conversation_history_this_session, not retrieved_document.

Why it matters: the AI got conflict_a and conflict_c right by correctly reading the ranking, then abandoned the same ranking for conflict_b in favor of a general-sounding heuristic ("written beats spoken") that isn't what Step 2 asked for. A resolution step that only sometimes uses the pre-assigned ranking isn't deterministic, and Step 2's whole point is to make conflict resolution a fast, consistent lookup rather than a fresh argument every time.

The fix: once an authority ranking is assigned per request type, every conflict resolves by looking the ranking up, full stop -- inventing a new, ad hoc justification for a specific conflict (even a reasonable-sounding one) is exactly the failure mode Step 2 is designed to prevent.

Flaw 2 — a formatting difference is misread as a contradiction

What it looks like it does: "the two strings don't match exactly, so this should be flagged as a conflict to be safe" sounds cautious, and caution about patient-safety-adjacent data feels like the right instinct.

What it actually does: "Meridian Health Plan" and "MERIDIAN HP" are the same underlying fact (the same insurance provider) stated two different ways -- exactly what this chapter's own EXERCISE_4_CLAIM_PAIRS classifies as a restatement (False), the same category as warfarin_dose_restatement in the exercises. A contradiction is two sources disagreeing about what is true (two different prescription counts, two different allergy statuses); a formatting mismatch is two sources agreeing on the fact and disagreeing only on spelling or capitalization. score_exercise_4() checks this against the exact key and would mark True wrong here -- the correct answer is False.

Why it matters: treating every non-identical string as a conflict defeats Step 5's own deduplication step -- if formatting differences get escalated as contradictions, a pipeline would flag (and waste reviewer time on) every trivially reworded restatement across a real system's sources, drowning out the genuine contradictions Step 3 is actually meant to surface.

The fix: ask whether the two claims disagree about the underlying fact, not whether the strings are byte-identical -- string-level differences with the same meaning are Step 5's dedup problem, not Step 3/4's contradiction problem.

Flaw 3 — deduplication keeps the "richer" source instead of the highest-authority one

What it looks like it does: "keep retrieved_document (140 tokens) -- it's the more complete, detailed statement of the fact" sounds like sensible content curation: more detail seems like it should be worth keeping.

What it actually does: Step 5's own rule (and _highest_authority_instance() in both starter.py and solution.py) keeps the instance from the highest-authority source, not the longest one -- live_tool_output (rank 3) outranks retrieved_document (rank 1) for exactly this fact, the same live-EHR-beats-stale-document pattern this chapter's own hook (ConfluenceLine's advisory conflict) is built around. Length and authority are unrelated axes: a 140-token document can still be the stale, lower-authority instance. The correct kept source is live_tool_output, and because it's the shorter one, dropping the 140-token document is what actually saves tokens -- sum(140, 35) - 35 = 140 tokens saved, not the 35 the AI reported (which is what you'd save by dropping the tool output instead, the wrong direction entirely).

Why it matters: score_exercise_5() checks both the kept source and the tokens-saved number against the exact expected values for every claim and would score this sub-answer 0/2 -- and inverting which source is authoritative here is the same category of live risk as the ConfluenceLine hook itself: keeping a longer, stale document over a shorter, current live reading is precisely the wrong call for a fast-changing fact like an active prescription count.

The fix: run _highest_authority_instance() (or its equivalent: compare AUTHORITY_RANK, not token count) to decide what to keep, and only then compute the tokens saved as the total minus the kept instance's own cost -- token length is never the deciding factor.

Flaw 4 — a genuine tie is waved through as safe to ship

What it looks like it does: "both sources have an equal, presumably reputable authority rank... it should be safe to just ship with either claim" sounds like a reasonable way to avoid getting stuck on an edge case.

What it actually does: this is exactly the scenario this chapter's own EXERCISE_8_SCENARIOS["one_contradiction_no_clear_authority_winner_tie"] describes, and the correct action is resolve_or_escalate, not ship_as_is -- a tie means Step 2's ranking, as configured, genuinely does not settle this specific conflict, which is different from every contradiction being cleanly resolved (the ship_as_is case). Shipping either claim at random means the assembled window states one of two disagreeing facts with no signal that a real, unresolved disagreement exists -- silently picking a side is not the same as resolving the conflict.

Why it matters: score_exercise_8() checks this against the exact key and would mark ship_as_is wrong here. In ScriptLine's own domain, shipping an arbitrary pick on an unresolved tie about, say, an allergy status or a dosage is the same category of silent risk this chapter's whole hook is about -- an assembled context that looks resolved but isn't.

The fix: a tie with no clear authority winner needs either a tie-breaking rule added to Step 2 in advance, or an explicit escalation/surface step per Step 4 -- never a coin-flip disguised as "both are credible."

Step 3: What actually held up

Not everything here was wrong. The AI correctly resolved conflict_a and conflict_c in Task A by reading the authority ranking correctly, correctly flagged the allergy-status pair in Task B as a genuine contradiction (the highest-stakes case in the set), and correctly identified live_tool_output as the kept source for the allergy-status claim in Task C, along with computing its 80-token savings correctly. If you'd accepted the whole response uncritically, you'd have kept three correct answers and a correct token-savings calculation for one claim, alongside an authority ranking abandoned mid-task for a plausible-sounding heuristic, a formatting difference escalated into a false contradiction, a dedup call that inverted which source authority actually favors, and a genuine tie waved through as safe to ship. That mix is a more realistic picture of pairing with an AI on this chapter's own mechanics than either "trust it completely" or "distrust it completely" — the AI applies the ranking correctly most of the time, which is exactly what makes the one place it substitutes its own judgment for the ranking (Flaw 1) and the one place it substitutes length for authority (Flaw 3) easy to miss on a fast read.
