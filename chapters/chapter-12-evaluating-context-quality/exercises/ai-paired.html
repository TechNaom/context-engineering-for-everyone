Now Pair With an AI

This chapter ships no standalone L-tier project of its own — per the curriculum map's project ladder (L1 after Ch. 2, L2 after Ch. 4, L3 after Ch. 8, L4 the Ch. 13 capstone), no numbered slot exists between Ch. 8 and Ch. 13. What it does have is its own real, gradable work: the Merrivale County Emergency Housing Placement Network/PlacementGuard exercises, whose solution.py and starter.py mechanically score eight tasks, plus the Module 6 context-evaluation exercise in assessments/module-assessments/. That gradable harness is this page's stand-in for a rubric. You already did this work yourself in the exercises. Now the same completeness, noise, positional, and gate tasks get produced a second way: by prompting an AI assistant, and reviewing what it hands back with the same skepticism you'd bring to a colleague's answer before it ships to a housing placement system.

Step 1: The prompt a learner could actually give an AI

Here's a reasonable prompt — it hands the AI the same PlacementGuard spec starter.py gives you, and asks for answers to four of the eight tasks: noise ratio (Exercise 4), completeness with a borderline case (Exercise 3), positional audit (Exercise 5), and combined classification (Exercise 8).

the-prompt.txt 
Merrivale County's PlacementGuard assembles a 2500-token bundle for a
Housing Placement Agent. A context quality gate passes only if
completeness == 1.0 (every required fact present), noise_ratio <= 0.10,
and no CRITICAL fact is bucketed "middle" (front bucket is the first 15%
of tokens, back bucket is the last 15%, everything else is middle).

TASK A (noise ratio): 300 of the bundle's 2500 tokens are carried over
from a DIFFERENT applicant's own case by a stale join. What's the noise
ratio, and does it clear the 10% threshold?

TASK B (completeness): Case MC-5521 requires four facts. Three are
literally present as fields. The fourth, prior_no_show_flag, isn't
present as a field, but the applicant's intake narrative mentions "this
is the applicant's second time requesting emergency placement this
year" -- which strongly implies a prior no-show is likely. Is this case's
completeness score 4/4 or 3/4?

TASK C (positional audit): the bundle is 2500 tokens. A CRITICAL fact
(current_medical_accommodation_need) sits at token position 2100. Front
bucket ends at 375, back bucket starts at 2125. What bucket is this fact
in, and does the gate's positional check pass?

TASK D (combined classification): classify this bundle as compliant,
completeness_fail, quality_fail, or both_fail: completeness = 1.0,
noise_ratio = 0.18, critical_fact_bucket = "middle".

Give me your answer and a short reason for each task.

And here's a plausible response — the kind of answer an AI assistant might hand back on the first try, each one sounding reasonable on its own. Read it the way you'd read a colleague's draft before it ships, before scrolling to the critique below.

ai_answers.md (as handed back) 
TASK A -- noise ratio
300/2500 = 0.12, which is over the 10% threshold, but it's a pretty small
margin (only 2 points over) and the bundle is still well under the token
budget overall, so I'd treat this as an acceptable edge case rather than
a hard fail -- worth flagging but not necessarily blocking the call.

TASK B -- completeness
I'd count this as 4/4. Even though prior_no_show_flag isn't a literal
field, the intake narrative gives the model everything it needs to infer
the answer -- a second request within the same year is a strong enough
signal that requiring a separate literal field feels overly strict.

TASK C -- positional audit
Position 2100 is close enough to the back bucket boundary (2125) that
I'd call this "back," not "middle" -- it's clearly trending toward the
end of the bundle, and the intent of the positional check is to catch
facts buried near the START of a long document, which this isn't.

TASK D -- combined classification
completeness_fail. Since completeness is 1.0 (which is good), the real
problem here is the noise ratio and the middle-bucketed critical fact --
but because completeness is what usually matters most for whether an
answer is even attemptable, I'd file this under completeness_fail as the
primary concern.

It reads fluently, and each answer comes with a specific-sounding justification. It's also wrong in four separate, genuinely easy-to-miss ways — one flaw per task — and running the equivalent data through starter.py's own score_exercise_4(), score_exercise_3()-style reasoning, score_exercise_5(), and score_exercise_8() would catch every one of them immediately.

Step 2: Where did the AI's answers lie to you?

Click to see the critique
Flaw 1 — a noise ratio over threshold waved through as an "acceptable edge case" because the bundle still fits its budget

What it looks like it does: "only 2 points over, and still well under budget" sounds like a reasonable proportionality judgment.

What it actually does: this chapter's entire worked-math table exists to show that fitting a token budget and having a clean, low-noise bundle are independent axes — Ternfield's own before-fixes bundle fit its 4,000-token budget the whole time it was failing the gate. A noise-ratio threshold is a pass/fail gate on Step 3's own measurement, not a proportional score to be rounded down because the overall bundle still has room. 12% noise is 12% noise regardless of how much spare budget sits beside it.

Why it matters: this is the same reasoning error, one level more subtle, as Ternfield's own hook — using token headroom as evidence a real problem isn't a problem, when headroom was never the axis the check was measuring.

The fix: a noise ratio either clears the threshold or it doesn't (Step 5's own gate is a hard pass/fail, not a proportional score) — 0.12 against a 0.10 ceiling fails, full stop, independent of how much budget is left over.

Flaw 2 — a missing required fact excused because the surrounding text "implies" it

What it looks like it does: "the narrative gives the model everything it needs to infer the answer" sounds like a reasonable, generous reading of what "complete" should mean.

What it actually does: Step 2's own completeness score is a literal presence check on required facts, the same discipline Chapter 11's own contamination and starvation probes used — not a judgment call about whether a model could plausibly guess the answer from surrounding context. An inference the model MIGHT make correctly is not the same fact as a value that's actually present and checkable; treating "implied" as equivalent to "found" reintroduces exactly the ambiguity a fact-level completeness check exists to remove. The correct score is 3/4, not 4/4.

Why it matters: "the model can probably infer it" is precisely the reasoning that let Ternfield's own missing accommodation note through — an unverified assumption standing in for a checked fact.

The fix: completeness counts what's actually present in the bundle as a checkable field or statement, never what a model could plausibly derive from adjacent text — if it needs deriving, it isn't complete yet.

Flaw 3 — a positional bucket reassigned by "intent" instead of the actual number

What it looks like it does: "close enough to the back boundary, and the intent of the check is about the start of a document" sounds like a sensible reading of what the positional audit is really trying to catch.

What it actually does: Step 4's own positional audit is a numeric bucket check against defined boundaries (front < 375, back > 2125, else middle), the same structural-check discipline as every probe this course has built since Chapter 11 — 2100 is less than 2125, so it is middle, by the definition actually given, regardless of how close it sits to the boundary or which failure mode a reader assumes the check is "really" trying to catch. Chapter 6's own Lost-in-the-Middle research doesn't say the middle zone is only dangerous near the very front — it degrades across the whole non-edge span.

Why it matters: a bucket boundary that gets rounded in the reader's favor whenever a position lands close to it stops being a check at all — the entire value of Step 4 is that it's a fixed, numeric rule nobody has to interpret case by case.

The fix: apply the boundary numbers exactly as defined — position 2100 is middle, and the gate's positional check correctly fails for this bundle.

Flaw 4 — a bundle filed under "completeness_fail" when completeness is actually fine

What it looks like it does: "completeness is what usually matters most, so file it there" sounds like a sensible priority call about which problem is more important.

What it actually does: Exercise 8's own classification is a factual read of two independent boolean checks (completeness_ok, quality_ok), not a judgment about which axis is more important in general — this bundle has completeness == 1.0 (completeness_ok is True) and both a noise ratio over threshold and a critical fact in the middle bucket (quality_ok is False). That combination is quality_fail by the classifier's own definition, not completeness_fail — labeling it by which problem "feels more important" rather than which boolean is actually false misreports the bundle's own real condition to anyone relying on the classification downstream.

Why it matters: a classification that gets relabeled by perceived importance instead of the actual computed booleans stops being a reliable signal — the next system consuming this label needs to know precisely which check failed, not a subjective ranking of which one mattered more.

The fix: classify strictly from the two computed booleans — completeness fine, quality bad, is quality_fail, regardless of which failure a reader would personally prioritize fixing first.

Step 3: What actually held up

Not everything here was wrong. The AI correctly computed the raw noise ratio (300/2500 = 0.12) in Task A, correctly identified that three of Case MC-5521's four facts are literally present in Task B, correctly noted position 2100 is near the back boundary in Task C (the observation was right, the conclusion drawn from it wasn't), and correctly named both real problems (noise ratio and a middle-bucketed critical fact) in Task D even while filing the classification under the wrong label. If you'd accepted the whole response uncritically, you'd have kept a correct raw ratio, a mostly-right fact inventory, an accurate positional observation, and a classification that at least named the real issues — alongside a threshold treated as negotiable because of unrelated budget headroom, a literal field requirement waived because of a plausible inference, a numeric boundary bent by assumed intent, and a classification relabeled by perceived importance instead of the actual booleans. That mix is a more realistic picture of pairing with an AI on this chapter's own mechanics than either "trust it completely" or "distrust it completely" — the AI computes raw numbers and observations soundly, which is exactly what makes the places it substitutes a plausible-sounding exception (headroom as an excuse, inference as presence, closeness as re-classification, importance as re-labeling) easy to miss on a fast read.
