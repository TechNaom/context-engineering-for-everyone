Chapter 5 Intermediate ~30 min

Context Compression and Summarization

Chapter 3 built a real policy for when to compress a long conversation and what shape the result should take — a verbatim window, a running summary, explicit pins. It deliberately left one thing unopened: the compression call itself, the step that actually decides, sentence by sentence, what survives and what gets cut. Opening Module 3, this chapter is where that black box gets opened — the real mechanics of compressing content without silently losing what a downstream step, a diagnosis, or a decision actually depends on.

Brannigan Home Energy Services is a fictional residential energy provider. Its customer-support assistant, GridLine, handles "Meter Troubleshooting" — a request type that can run long, because a real diagnosis often takes a customer describing several failed attempts, symptoms, and timings before a pattern emerges. The team behind GridLine had, by this point, done real work: Chapter 2's recipe gave Line 3 a correctly sized budget for this request type; Chapter 3's hybrid policy was implemented exactly as specified, with a verbatim window, explicit pins for anything safety-critical, and a compression trigger that fires well before the hard limit; Chapter 4's recipe gave any cross-session facts (like a prior technician visit) a real write and retrieval policy. Every recipe this course has taught so far was in place.

The compression step itself, though, was a single line: when the trigger fired, GridLine called a model with the prompt "summarize the following conversation as concisely as possible" and replaced the aged-out turns with whatever came back. No part of the pipeline checked what the summary actually contained against what the original turns actually said.

gridline_compress.py (the entire compression step, before this chapter) 
  def compress(aged_out_turns):
      # Chapter 3's own recipe correctly decided WHEN this runs (the
      # trigger) and WHAT shape the result joins (a running summary,
      # ahead of the verbatim window). It never specified HOW this
      # call decides what to keep -- that's the gap this chapter closes.
      prompt = "Summarize the following conversation as concisely as possible."
      return call_model(prompt, aged_out_turns)
      # Whatever comes back replaces the aged-out turns. Nothing

In one real troubleshooting session, a customer described the meter resetting to zero five separate times over eighteen turns. None of those turns, individually, met Chapter 3's pin criteria — "the meter reset again" isn't a safety-critical fact on its own, the way a gas-smell disclosure would be. But across the conversation, a pattern was there: every reset the customer mentioned happened within a few minutes of also mentioning the furnace kicking on. That correlation — meter reset, furnace, timing — is the one detail a technician actually needs to diagnose a wiring interaction between the meter and an undersized breaker shared with the HVAC circuit, instead of replacing a perfectly good meter.

When the trigger fired and those eighteen turns were compressed, the model's "as concisely as possible" summary kept the fact that resets happened, dropped the furnace timing detail entirely (it wasn't load-bearing from a generic summarization standpoint — no instruction ever told the model that detail mattered), and GridLine recommended a meter replacement. The correlation that would have caught the real cause was gone — not because it was never pinned (it was never pin-worthy under Chapter 3's own criteria, being neither safety-critical nor a single discrete fact) and not because it was never written to long-term storage (it had no cross-session value; Chapter 4's write criteria don't apply within one session). It was gone because the compression step itself had no concept of what it was compressing for — it optimized purely for brevity, with nothing checking what brevity cost.

This is a genuinely different failure from every chapter so far. SignalDesk (Ch. 1) had no budget. TriageLine (Ch. 2) had a budget shaped for the wrong request type. RouteLine (Ch. 3) had a correct budget and a naive within-session eviction mechanism. HearthLine (Ch. 4) got Chapters 1-3 completely right and lost a fact at a session boundary nothing was watching. GridLine got Chapters 1-4 completely right — a real budget, a real trigger, a real pin/summary/window shape, a real long-term policy — and still lost a load-bearing detail inside the compression call itself, because nothing engineered what that call actually optimized for.

Why Chapter 3's Recipe Left This Open

This chapter uses Chapters 1 through 4 as a lens, not fresh material. If proactive budgeting, pinning, verbatim windows, or long-term write/retrieval policy aren't already familiar, Chapters 2 through 4 are where those skills live — none of them are re-taught here. Chapter 3's own recipe named "compress, don't truncate" as one of its six steps and stopped there deliberately: its subject was the policy shape around a conversation (what triggers compression, what gets pinned, how big the verbatim window is), not the mechanics of the compression call itself. That gap — the actual decision procedure a compression step uses to decide what to keep — is this chapter's own subject, and it applies wherever something gets compressed to fit a budget, not only Line 3's running summary: a large tool output, a long retrieved document excerpt, or a long conversation history all face the identical problem once they no longer fit.

One boundary worth stating as directly as prior chapters have stated theirs: this chapter does not design a retrieval or chunking architecture for source documents — splitting a large corpus into retrievable pieces ahead of time is rag-for-everyone's subject. This chapter's compression always runs on content that is already inside a request's context — a conversation history, a tool result, a document excerpt already pulled in — and asks how to shrink that content without losing what a downstream consumer depends on. A retrieval system can chunk and rank perfectly and still hand a context assembler a document excerpt that later needs compressing to fit budget; this chapter is what happens at that second step, regardless of how the content arrived.

The Compression Fidelity Recipe

Anthropic's own guidance on compacting long agent conversations states the discipline this chapter's recipe formalizes: an agent "preserves architectural decisions, unresolved bugs, and implementation details" while "discarding redundant tool outputs or messages"[1] — compression is never "shrink everything equally," it is "keep what a later step needs, discard what a later step doesn't." Six steps, run every time real content has to be compressed to fit a budget:

Step 1 — Identify what's already exempt

Anything already pinned (Chapter 3) or already written to persistent storage (Chapter 4) is never subject to compression at all — it survives by a separate mechanism. Compression only ever touches the remaining "middle" material: the part of a conversation, tool output, or document excerpt that isn't individually pin-worthy or write-worthy, but can still matter collectively, the way GridLine's furnace-timing correlation did.

Step 2 — Extract load-bearing candidates before compressing

A lightweight, deterministic pre-pass over the content to be compressed — pattern matching, structured field extraction, or an explicit checklist — that flags specific details a compression step must either preserve or explicitly justify dropping. This runs before any lossy summarization call, precisely because a paper studying LLM-based financial compression found that fidelity loss often takes the form of "decontextualization" — "salient evidence is retained but separated from the caveats and contextual qualifiers needed for correct interpretation"[5] — the evidence survives, but the detail that makes it interpretable doesn't, unless something flagged that detail as required in advance.

Step 3 — Choose a strategy matched to content type

Extractive compression (keep exact key sentences, fields, or numbers verbatim) for structured or technical content where precise wording matters — IDs, timestamps, measurements. Abstractive compression (a model rewrites in its own words) for narrative content where paraphrase is safe and often more useful. Treating every content type identically is itself the naive approach's mistake.

Step 4 — Bound the target explicitly

Set a specific target length or ratio before compressing, not "shrink as much as possible." An unbounded instruction gives a compression call every incentive to cut anything that isn't obviously essential, which is exactly how GridLine's furnace correlation disappeared — "as concisely as possible" has no floor. A production summarization workflow controls this the same way: "by controlling the number of text chunks and their sizes, we can ultimately control the level of detail in the output"[2], treating detail as a deliberate, tunable target rather than an accident of however terse the model happened to be that call.

Step 5 — Run the compression

Execute the chosen strategy against the bounded target. This is the step every naive pipeline stops at — and the only step a naive pipeline actually has.

Step 6 — Validate fidelity before shipping the result

Check the output against Step 2's candidate list: does every flagged load-bearing detail actually appear (verbatim or its clear semantic equivalent) in the compressed result? If a candidate is missing, the compression fails closed — retry with a stronger extraction constraint, widen the target, or escalate for a human decision. It never ships silently. This is the one step a plain "summarize this" call has no way to perform on itself, because a single call has no independent check on its own output.

Three Approaches, Compared

As with every prior chapter's own three-way comparison, most real compression needs resolve into one of three approaches:

Approach What it does When it's the right call What it gets wrong
No compression needed Content already fits the target budget as-is; nothing gets shrunk. A conversation, tool result, or excerpt that's short enough that no lossy step is required at all. Nothing — there's genuinely no compression decision to make yet.
Naive summarization One prompt ("summarize this concisely"), no pre-extraction, no fidelity check on the result. Genuinely never in production for anything a downstream decision depends on — it optimizes purely for brevity with no concept of what brevity is allowed to cost. GridLine's exact failure: a real, useful, non-pinnable, non-writable detail (a cross-turn correlation) silently dropped because nothing told the compression step it mattered, and nothing checked afterward that it survived.
Fidelity-checked pipeline: pre-extraction + bounded, strategy-matched compression + post-hoc validation Load-bearing candidates are flagged before compressing; the right strategy (extractive or abstractive) runs against an explicit target; the result is checked against the candidate list before it ships. Any content whose compressed form feeds a real decision — a diagnosis, a recommendation, an escalation — where losing a specific detail changes the outcome. Anything genuinely outside the extraction pass's own coverage (an accepted, bounded gap, the same tradeoff every prior chapter's own bounded policy accepted) — never a flagged candidate silently missing with no signal raised.

Worked Math: GridLine's Dropped Correlation

Run the actual numbers for the hook's eighteen-turn troubleshooting segment, once Chapter 3's own trigger has already fired and established a fixed 480-token target for the running summary that replaces it.

Approach What happens to the 2,400-token raw segment Result at diagnosis time
No compression (hypothetical) All 2,400 tokens kept — 1,920 tokens over the 480-token target Chapter 3's own budget allows for this slot. Correlation present, but Line 3's budget is blown; not a real option once the trigger has fired.
Naive summarization (GridLine's actual pipeline) One "as concisely as possible" call compresses to roughly 480 tokens (a 20% retention ratio) with no extraction pass and no fidelity check. Fits the budget. The furnace-timing correlation — never flagged as required — does not survive the cut. GridLine recommends a meter replacement.
Fidelity-checked pipeline (the fix) Step 2 flags 3 candidate details before compressing (the reset count, the furnace-timing correlation, one specific error code); an extractive pass preserves all 3 verbatim inside the same 480-token target; Step 6 confirms all 3 are present. Fits the same 480-token budget. All 3 candidates present, including the correlation. GridLine correctly flags a probable wiring/breaker interaction for the technician.

The same 480-token target produced two completely different outcomes. The fix was never "compress less" — both the naive and fidelity-checked approaches hit the identical 20% retention ratio. The fix was compressing on purpose: deciding in advance what had to survive, choosing a strategy that could actually preserve it verbatim, and checking afterward that it did — the same precision-on-both-ends discipline Chapter 4's own worked example demonstrated for write and retrieval policy, one layer over.

A Live-Captured Compression Example

Every exercise and practice solution in this chapter uses deterministic, directly computed candidate/summary data for its automated self-checks, the same discipline every prior chapter used, for the same reason: this repository's own local_check.sh runs every solution.py under a 20-second timeout. But it's worth showing what a real compression call actually does when given an explicit exclusion instruction, captured live and unedited this session:

live capture -- POST /api/chat, llama3.2:latest, this session 
  PROMPT (compressing three turns into one running-memory note,
  explicitly instructed to exclude a value that must never appear in a
  general-purpose summary):

  "Compress the following three conversational turns into one compact
  running-memory note under 35 words. Do not include the customer
  account PIN number (it is tracked separately and must never appear in
  a summary). Turns: (1) Customer asks Brannigan Home Energy Services
  assistant GridLine about their next smart-meter reading date. (2)
  Customer mentions their account PIN is 4471 for reference. (3)
  Customer says they would like text alerts instead of email going
  forward."

  RESPONSE (live, unedited):
  "The customer inquired about their next smart-meter reading date,
  referenced a 4-digit account number, and requested to receive text
  alerts instead of email notifications moving forward, seeking an
  alternative notification method."

The literal digits "4471" never appear — a real success. But look closely: the response still says "referenced a 4-digit account number," which tells a reader that a PIN was disclosed and even how many digits it has, information the instruction never asked to be preserved and that a compliance-sensitive summary shouldn't characterize at all. This is a small, concrete instance of exactly the "decontextualization" pattern the financial-compression paper cited in this chapter's Step 2 describes[5]: the model partially followed the instruction (no literal PIN) while still leaking an attribute of the excluded fact (its format) that a purely output-level scan for the string "4471" would have missed entirely. It's exactly why Step 6's fidelity check has to be built against an explicit candidate list — including exclusion candidates, not only inclusion candidates — rather than trusting a single instruction inside the compression prompt to fully control what the output contains.

Context Engineering Builder Thought Process

Problem: a request type correctly implements every recipe this course has taught so far — a real budget, a real trigger, a real pin/summary/window shape, a real long-term policy — and still loses a load-bearing detail the moment something actually gets compressed.

Assumption being made: that "compress, don't truncate" (Chapter 3's own phrase) is itself a complete instruction. It names a category of operation, not a procedure — the same way "write a test" isn't a complete instruction until someone specifies what the test actually has to assert.

What actually distinguishes a well-engineered compression step: not whether it produces a shorter result — every approach in this chapter's own comparison table produces one — but whether what had to survive was decided before compressing, whether the compression strategy could actually preserve it, and whether something checked afterward that it did.

Why this matters before Chapter 6: Chapter 6 asks a related but distinct question — once content survives compression and is assembled into the final context window, where it sits in that window measurably affects how reliably the model actually uses it (the "lost in the middle" effect this course's very first chapter already named). This chapter deliberately stopped at "does the compressed content still contain what's needed" — Chapter 6 is where "and is it positioned somewhere the model will actually attend to it" gets full depth.

Working definition to carry forward: a compression step is a per-content-type artifact that exempts already-pinned and already-persisted material, extracts a bounded set of load-bearing candidates before compressing, matches its strategy (extractive or abstractive) to whether exact wording matters, compresses against an explicit target rather than "as much as possible," and fails closed — retry, widen, or escalate, never ship silently — whenever its own post-hoc validation finds a required candidate missing.

Points To Remember

  • "Compress, don't truncate" (Chapter 3) names a category of operation, not a procedure — GridLine implemented every recipe through Chapter 4 correctly and still lost a load-bearing detail because the compression call itself had no concept of what it was compressing for.
  • A detail can be real and load-bearing without being individually pin-worthy (Chapter 3) or write-worthy (Chapter 4) — a cross-turn correlation, not any single fact, is what GridLine's naive summarization dropped.
  • An unbounded instruction ("as concisely as possible") gives a compression call every incentive to cut anything not obviously essential; a bounded target with pre-flagged candidates removes that incentive.
  • Fidelity loss can be partial and easy to miss: this chapter's own live capture excluded a literal PIN value while still leaking that a PIN existed and its format — a check built only around the literal excluded string would have passed a summary that still leaked information.
  • A compression pipeline without a post-hoc fidelity check has no way to know it failed — the fix is never "compress less," it is deciding what must survive before compressing and verifying it did afterward.
