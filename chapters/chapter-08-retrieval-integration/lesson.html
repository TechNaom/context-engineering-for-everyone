Retrieval Integration: From Ranked Results to Context

Chapter 7 opened Module 4 by asking how several already-produced sources combine into one window without contradicting or crowding each other out. It deliberately treated every candidate source — including a retrieved document — as already well-formed by the time it reached the inventory step. This chapter, closing Module 4, owns the handoff Chapter 7 left open on purpose: a retriever doesn't hand back a well-formed source. It hands back a ranked list of scored chunks, and something has to decide which of them actually earn a place in the window, how they're stitched back together, and what they carry with them — before Chapter 7's own recipe ever gets to treat "the retrieved document" as one clean, single entry in its inventory.

Mossgate Regional Law Library Consortium is a fictional multi-branch legal research library. Its research assistant, CiteLine, helps attorneys and clerks answer case-law questions by retrieving the most relevant excerpts from a large opinion corpus and handing them, ranked by relevance score, to a model. By the time this chapter's story starts, the retriever itself — the embedding model, the similarity search, the ranking — was working exactly as designed. That is deliberately not this chapter's problem: `rag-for-everyone` already owns retrieval architecture, chunking, embeddings, and ranking quality, and this chapter assumes all of it as a correct, given input, exactly as it names later in this lesson.

A clerk asked CiteLine whether the excited-utterance hearsay exception applied to a witness statement made twenty-five minutes after a car crash, with no evidence the witness was still under acute stress when they spoke. The retriever did its job well: its top-ranked chunk, scored 0.91, was the exact controlling paragraph from Mossgate v. Harrow County (2019) stating the general rule. Its second-ranked chunk, scored 0.88, was the very next paragraph of the same opinion — the one stating the rule's own ten-minute qualifying limit, the fact that actually decides this exact question. Three lower-ranked chunks, scored 0.42, 0.39, and 0.35, were excerpts from two unrelated opinions about business records and expert-witness qualification.

citeline_retrieve.py (retrieval hand-off, before this chapter) 
  def build_retrieved_context(query, budget_tokens=500):
      ranked_chunks = retriever.search(query, top_k=5)  # correct, well-tested
      # ranked_chunks == [(0.91, chunk_a, "doc_mossgate_v_harrow"),
      #                    (0.88, chunk_b, "doc_mossgate_v_harrow"),
      #                    (0.42, chunk_c, "doc_talbot_v_state"),
      #                    (0.39, chunk_d, "doc_renwick_v_ostgate"),
      #                    (0.35, chunk_e, "doc_unrelated_5")]
      text = ""
      for score, chunk, doc_id in ranked_chunks:
          # Always take top_k=5, no matter how the scores actually look.
          # No relevance floor. No budget-aware selection -- just append
          # until the raw character count runs out mid-sentence. No record
          # of which doc a chunk came from once it's in the string. No
          # check for whether chunk_a and chunk_b are the very next
          # paragraph of the same opinion and belong stitched together.
          text += chunk + "\n\n"
      return text[:budget_tokens * 4]  # crude char-based cutoff, mid-chunk

Under the 500-token budget this request type actually had, that crude cutoff kept the full 0.91 chunk, most of the 0.88 chunk, and then ran out of room — truncating the ten-minute qualifying clause right where it mattered most, mid-sentence, with no signal to anything downstream that the cut had happened or that a second, directly-connected paragraph had even existed. CiteLine's answer confidently stated the exception applied, citing only the general rule, and never mentioned the ten-minute limit at all — not because the retriever failed to find it (it found it, and ranked it second out of five), but because nothing between the retriever and the model decided that a fragment cut off mid-clause is worse than no fragment at all, or that the two top chunks were the same contiguous paragraph and belonged together.

Why Chapter 7 Left This Open on Purpose

This chapter uses Chapters 1 through 7 as a lens, not fresh material — if the budget ledger, memory policies, the Compression Fidelity Recipe, the Context Ordering Recipe, or the Source Assembly Recipe aren't already familiar, Chapters 2 through 7 are where those skills live, and none of them are re-taught here. Chapter 7's own Source Assembly Recipe starts at Step 1 by inventorying "every candidate source" — but it never asked what makes a retrieved document a well-formed candidate source in the first place. It assumed CiteLine's retrieved chunks arrived already thresholded, already budget-fit, already carrying their own provenance, already stitched into coherent blocks. That assumption is exactly what this chapter builds, closing Module 4.

Two boundaries worth naming as directly as Chapter 7's own lesson named its boundary against Chapter 6: this chapter does not teach retrieval architecture, chunking strategy, embedding choice, or ranking-model quality — how the five scored chunks in the hook above got produced, and whether a different retriever would have scored them differently, is `rag-for-everyone`'s own subject, out of scope here entirely. And this chapter does not decide how a well-formed retrieved source stacks up against other source types — a live tool call, the conversation, system instructions — when they disagree; that is Chapter 7's own Source Assembly Recipe, already built, and this chapter's own output is designed to be handed straight to it as one inventoried source, not a replacement for it. This chapter's own job sits in between: given a retriever's already-correct ranked, scored output, how does it become the one well-formed candidate source Chapter 7's Step 1 is entitled to assume it already has?

The Retrieval Integration Recipe

Six steps, run on a retriever's raw ranked output before it is handed to Chapter 7's own Source Assembly Recipe as a single inventoried source:

Step 1 — Apply a relevance floor before selecting anything

Don't select "the top k" by definition — select chunks that clear a minimum relevance score for this request type. CiteLine's actual pipeline took top_k=5 unconditionally, so the three unrelated chunks (0.42, 0.39, 0.35) were never actually excluded on relevance; they were just low-ranked. A relevance floor is a decision about what counts as "relevant enough to include," made once per request type, not an artifact of whatever k happened to be configured.

Step 2 — Fit the surviving chunks to budget by score, not by truncating mid-chunk

Once Step 1 has dropped what doesn't clear the floor, select among what remains by score until the retrieval budget (Chapter 2's own recipe, a given input here) is spent, and stop at a chunk boundary — never truncate a surviving chunk mid-sentence to make it "just fit." CiteLine's actual failure was exactly a mid-clause cut of its second-highest-scored, most load-bearing chunk, which a boundary-aware fit would never have produced.

Step 3 — Preserve each surviving chunk's provenance

Carry the source document, section, and score alongside each chunk's text, not just the raw string. Anthropic's own context-engineering guidance frames the general principle directly: context should be the "smallest possible set of high-signal tokens that maximize the likelihood of the desired outcome"[1] — and an unattributed chunk of text is lower-signal than the same chunk with its source attached, because nothing downstream (a citation, an authority ranking, a human reviewer) can verify or rank it without knowing where it came from. This is also what makes Chapter 7's own Step 2 authority ranking possible to apply to a retrieved chunk at all.

Step 4 — Stitch adjacent chunks from the same document back together

When two or more surviving chunks are consecutive passages of the same source document, merge them into a single contiguous block rather than leaving them as separate fragments. CiteLine's 0.91 and 0.88 chunks were literally the same paragraph and the one immediately following it in the same opinion — chunking during retrieval defaults to a mechanical, fixed-size character split by design (LangChain's own text-splitter documentation states directly that "for most use cases, start with the RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter"[2], i.e. mechanical character boundaries as the default starting point, not semantic ones), so nothing about the chunk boundary itself reflects where one legal point ends and the qualifying limit on it begins. Re-stitching is this chapter's own job precisely because the retriever's own boundaries were never meant to be semantic boundaries.

Step 5 — Handle a low-confidence or empty result set explicitly

When nothing clears Step 1's relevance floor, that is itself a real, distinct outcome — surface it as "no sufficiently relevant result found" rather than silently falling back to the highest-ranked chunk anyway because a well-formed context block is expected downstream. Google Cloud's own Agent Search documentation confirms this exact failure has a name and a fix in production retrieval systems: a configurable relevance threshold (HIGH/MEDIUM/LOW/LOWEST) below which a retrieved result is excluded rather than passed through[3] — the underlying problem (a low-relevance chunk arriving as if it were a genuine answer) is common enough that major vendors ship a dedicated knob for it.

Step 6 — Hand the resolved, provenance-tagged bundle to Chapter 7's own Source Assembly Recipe

This chapter decides what a well-formed retrieved source looks like; Chapter 7's Source Assembly Recipe, already built, decides how that one source (now correctly typed retrieved_document, complete with provenance) stacks up against every other candidate source, and Chapter 6's own recipe after that decides where the result sits. Skipping straight from a raw ranked chunk list to Chapter 7's inventory step, the way CiteLine's actual pipeline did, hands Chapter 7 a source it was never designed to receive.

Three Approaches, Compared

Approach What it does When it's the right call What it gets wrong
Unconditional top-k stuffing (CiteLine's actual pipeline) Always takes exactly k ranked chunks, appends them in score order, then truncates by raw character count wherever the budget runs out. Never, once a request type can plausibly return chunks whose relevance drops off sharply within the top k — which is most real retrieval, not an edge case. CiteLine's exact failure: three genuinely irrelevant chunks always included by definition of "top 5," and its single most load-bearing chunk truncated mid-clause because the cutoff never respected a chunk boundary.
Relevance-floor filtering only Drops chunks below a minimum score before selection, but has no budget-aware boundary-safe fit, no provenance tracking, and no adjacent-chunk stitching. A real, worthwhile improvement over unconditional top-k — worth doing regardless — but not sufficient alone. Correctly drops CiteLine's three unrelated chunks, but does nothing about the mid-clause truncation of the surviving 0.88 chunk, and nothing about the fact that the two surviving chunks are one continuous paragraph split in two.
The Retrieval Integration Recipe (the fix) Applies a relevance floor (Step 1), fits surviving chunks to budget at a chunk boundary (Step 2), attaches provenance to each (Step 3), stitches adjacent same-document chunks together (Step 4), handles an empty/low-confidence result explicitly (Step 5), and hands the resolved bundle to Chapter 7's own recipe (Step 6). Any request type where a retriever's raw ranked output can plausibly contain irrelevant chunks, a mid-clause truncation risk, or split contiguous passages — true of nearly every real RAG pipeline operating under a real token budget. Requires deciding a relevance floor and a stitching rule in advance for every request type — real design work, the same category Chapters 2 and 7 already required for budget sizing and authority ranking, not a new class of cost.

Worked Math: Mossgate's Dropped Qualifying Clause

Per Chapter 2's own recipe, CiteLine's "Case Law Lookup" request type has a 500-token retrieval budget for this turn. The retriever returned five ranked chunks:

Chunk Score Tokens Document
A0.91180Mossgate v. Harrow County, Sec. 4.2 (general rule)
B0.88160Mossgate v. Harrow County, Sec. 4.3 (ten-minute limit — the paragraph immediately following A)
C0.42200Talbot v. State (unrelated, business records)
D0.39150Renwick v. Ostgate (unrelated, expert witness)
E0.35190Unrelated fifth opinion
Approach What survives, and how Result
Unconditional top-5 (actual pipeline) All 880 raw tokens (180+160+200+150+190) get appended in order, then crudely cut at 500 tokens by character count: chunk A survives whole (180), chunk B is cut at 320 tokens in, keeping only 140 of its 160 tokens — losing the ten-minute qualifying clause, which sits in B's final sentences. The assembled 500-token block contains the general rule in full, an amputated fragment of the one paragraph that actually answers the clerk's question, and zero of the (correctly excluded in spirit, but never actually excluded) unrelated content C/D/E ever got the chance to matter — the real damage was entirely from the crude, boundary-blind cutoff.
Relevance-floor filtering only (floor = 0.5) Chunks C, D, E (0.42, 0.39, 0.35) are correctly dropped before selection: 340 tokens (180+160) remain, comfortably inside the 500-token budget, so no truncation is even needed this time. Both A and B survive in full, so the ten-minute clause is present — but the two chunks are represented as two separate, un-stitched blocks with no marker that B is the direct continuation of A, and neither carries a citation a reader could actually verify against the opinion.
Retrieval Integration Recipe (the fix) Step 1 drops C/D/E at the 0.5 floor. Step 2 confirms A+B's 340 tokens fit the 500-token budget with no truncation needed. Step 3 tags the merged block with its provenance: Mossgate v. Harrow County (2019), Sec. 4.2-4.3. Step 4 stitches A and B into one 340-token contiguous excerpt, since they are consecutive paragraphs of the same opinion. Step 5 doesn't apply (a real, non-empty result survived Step 1). A single, complete, citable 340-token excerpt containing both the general rule and its qualifying limit as one continuous passage — 160 tokens under budget, versus the naive pipeline's exact-budget block that still managed to lose the one clause that decided the answer.

The fix was never "retrieve fewer candidates" or "raise top_k" (both are retrieval-architecture tuning, `rag-for-everyone`'s subject, not this one) or "raise the budget" (Chapter 2's subject, and the budget was already generous enough at 340 of 500 tokens once the irrelevant chunks were correctly excluded). The fix was deciding, on purpose, what counts as relevant enough to keep, where it is and isn't safe to cut when a budget is tight, and that two chunks from the same document, sitting right next to each other in the source text, are one passage — not three separate integration decisions the retriever itself was ever responsible for making.

A genuinely nuanced second result, read honestly rather than graded pass/fail. Without the ten-minute clause in context at all, the model did not confidently misstate the rule the way CiteLine's own hook-story failure did — it correctly noticed the excerpt it had was incomplete on the timing question. But its actual path there was still a real problem: it reasoned its way to a plausible- sounding conclusion ("the stress of excitement may not have been present") that is not stated anywhere in Excerpt 1, effectively inventing a rationale the retrieved text never provided, rather than stating plainly that the provided excerpts don't answer a timing question at all. This is a precise, concrete illustration of why Step 2's boundary-safe fit and Step 4's stitching matter even when a model doesn't fail as visibly as CiteLine's own hook story: a missing load-bearing clause doesn't just risk a confidently wrong answer, it can also produce a confident-sounding answer built on reasoning that was never actually grounded in anything retrieved.

Context Engineering Builder Thought Process

Problem: a retriever returns correctly ranked, correctly scored chunks — retrieval architecture itself did its job — and the request type still fails, either by confidently omitting the fact that actually decides the answer (CiteLine's own hook story) or by fabricating plausible-sounding reasoning to fill a gap a missing chunk left behind (this chapter's own second live capture).

Assumption being made: that "the retriever returned the right chunks, in the right order" is the same claim as "the context handed to the model is well-formed." Chapter 7 already showed correct retrieval per source isn't the same as coherent combination across sources; this chapter shows correct ranking within a single retriever's own output isn't the same as a well-formed single source, before combination with anything else is even in play.

What actually distinguishes a well-engineered retrieval hand-off: not whether the retriever itself found the right passages (every approach in this chapter's own comparison table achieves that) but whether a relevance floor was applied on purpose, whether the budget fit respected chunk boundaries instead of truncating mid-clause, whether provenance survived into the final block, whether adjacent same-document chunks were recognized and stitched, and whether an empty or low-confidence result was surfaced honestly instead of papered over.

Why this closes Module 4: Chapter 7 assumed a well-formed retrieved source as a given input to its own Step 1 inventory; this chapter is what makes that assumption true in the first place. Together, Chapters 7 and 8 cover the module's own two labs completely: assembling multiple already-well-formed sources without contradiction (Ch. 7), and turning one retriever's raw ranked output into a well-formed source before it ever reaches that inventory (Ch. 8) — neither chapter re-derives the other's own recipe, and this chapter's own project, closing the module, is built to require both.

Working definition to carry forward: a well-integrated retrieved source applies a relevance floor before selecting anything, fits surviving chunks to budget at a chunk boundary rather than a character count, preserves each chunk's provenance, stitches adjacent same-document chunks into coherent passages, surfaces an empty or low-confidence result explicitly, and only then is handed to Chapter 7's own Source Assembly Recipe as a single, well-formed candidate source — never assumed well-formed just because the underlying ranking was correct.

Points To Remember

  • A retriever returning correctly ranked, correctly scored chunks is not the same as a well-formed context source — the two live captures this session showed a missing load-bearing clause can produce either a confidently wrong answer or a confident-sounding but ungrounded one.
  • Unconditional top-k stuffing includes chunks by definition, not by relevance, and truncates by raw character count with no awareness of chunk boundaries — CiteLine's actual pipeline cut its single most load-bearing chunk mid-clause this way.
  • The fix is a deterministic six-step recipe, not retriever tuning: apply a relevance floor, fit to budget at a chunk boundary, preserve provenance, stitch adjacent same-document chunks, handle an empty/low-confidence result explicitly, and hand the result to Chapter 7's own Source Assembly Recipe as one well-formed source.
  • Relevance-floor filtering alone is a real, worthwhile improvement over unconditional top-k — but it does not fix mid-clause truncation or un-stitched, disconnected adjacent chunks on its own.
  • Chunk boundaries produced during retrieval are mechanical (fixed-size or fixed-character splits), not semantic — nothing about where a retriever happened to cut reflects where one legal, medical, or policy point ends and its own qualifying limit begins.
